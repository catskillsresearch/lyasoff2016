Another look at the integral of exponential Brownian
motion and the pricing of Asian options

Andrew Lyasoff1




Received: 19 February 2015 / Accepted: 8 April 2016 / Published online: 15 July 2016
© Springer-Verlag Berlin Heidelberg 2016



Abstract It is shown that Marc Yor’s formula (Adv. Appl. Probab. 24:509–531,
1992) for the density of the integral of exponential Brownian motion taken over a
finite time interval is an extremal member of a family of previously unknown in-
tegral formulae for the same density. The derivation is independent from the one
by Yor and obtained from a simple time-reversibility feature, in conjunction with a
Fokker–Planck type argument. Similar arguments lead to an independent derivation
of Dufresne’s result (Scand. Actuar. J. 90:39–79, 1990) for the law of the integral
taken over an infinite time interval. The numerical aspects of the new formulae are
developed, with concrete applications to Asian options.

Keywords Exponential Brownian motion · Random environment · Asian options

for a comprehensive bibliography, we refer the reader to the survey papers of Mat-

counting Yor’s original result [33], which we are going to obtain as a special case
of more general integral formulae, the only other known result that crops up in our
study—accidentally, and seemingly out of nowhere—is the striking identity in (4.4).

Remark 3.1 The above formula can be traced back to Pollak and Siegmund [25,
Proposition 3], where it is established in the special case ν = − 12 σ . In its present
form, it was first derived by Dufresne [11] by using an entirely different method.
Since then, a number of other derivations have emerged: Yor [34], Bertoin and Yor
[3, (16)], Matsumoto and Yor [22, p. 334], Baudoin and O’Connell [2, (6)], etc. Ex-
cept for [2], which relies on the features of the Laplace transform of the integral, the
consensus strategy appears to be to derive the law of the integral over infinite time as
the limit of the law of the integral over finite time,3 which in turn can be identified
in law with a diffusion process that has the same dynamics as the process Z (one can
write the same SDE for both) and shares the same generator as the one in (3.2). The
shortcut in the foregoing is in the explicit construction of a process (Z from equation
(3.1)) that already is stationary (immediately after it starts at t = 0), has the same law
(for any fixed t, including t = 0) as the integral taken at infinite time period, and is
subject to a Markovian dynamics described in terms of a concrete generator (L from
equation (3.2)). This allows us to circumvent the passing-to-the-limit step in the argu-
ment, and to immediately conclude that the “running” (as opposed to the “limiting”)
density of the already stationary process must solve the associated Fokker–Planck
equation, without the need to invoke any tools from diffusion theory in order to jus-
tify the ergodicity feature and connect the infinitesimal generator with the limiting
law.4

6 Computation of expected values and applications to Asian options

Because the initial motivation for the present study was to achieve greater com-
putational tractability, our exposition would not be complete without concrete ex-
amples to illustrate the applicability of the new formulae we have developed. As
Another look at the integral of exponential Brownian motion                                         1089

the most widely discussed concrete examples documented throughout the litera-
ture (related to the distribution of the integral of exponential Brownian motion) in-
volve the pricing of Asian options, this will be the nature of our illustrations also.
However, we emphasize very strongly that the essence of our approach is to carry
out calculations by using the density of the distribution directly, and in this re-
spect our method can be seen as universal, in the sense that the concrete repre-
sentations for the density that we obtain are not related to Asian options in any
way.
   The numerical evaluation of the prices of Asian options has a long history and
can be traced back at least as far as Turnbull and Wakeman [28]. The next step
was due to Eydeland and Geman [14], who used a particular algorithm for invert-
ing the Laplace transform (in the time variable) which was computed by Geman
and Yor [15]. Another widely used approach to Asian options is to establish lower
and upper bounds for the price. This method was pioneered by Rogers and Shi [26],
and we refer to the recent work of Kordzakhia and Novikov [18] for a unified and
expanded framework as well as an up-to-date overview of this approach and the re-
lated literature. Generically, the upper/lower bound technique can give the price of
the Asian option with 3 decimal places of accuracy. Other computational methods,
specifically designed for Asian options, have been developed by Dufresne [12, 13],
Milevsky and Posner [24], Thompson [27], Linetsky [19], Večeř [29, 30] and others.
Among these works, Linetsky’s provides the most accurate results, achieving 10 dec-
imal places of accuracy. Since his numerical examples are benchmarked against those
of his predecessors, we use in this section his parameter values (in all 7 cases from
[19, Table 1]) as a benchmark test, but include 24 new cases at the end. We stress,
however, that the methodology developed in the present paper is not specific to Asian
options.
